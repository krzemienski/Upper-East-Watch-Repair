Jewelry repair prices in New York City make more sense when you separate the visible problem from the work required to correct it. A broken chain may need one clean solder joint, or it may reveal thin links that will not safely hold a new joint. A ring that feels tight may be straightforward to size, or it may have stones and surface treatments that call for extra handling. The ranges below are broad 2026 NYC planning figures, not guaranteed prices from our shop or any other jeweler. A sound estimate starts with an in-person inspection.

## Typical jewelry repair cost ranges in NYC

These ranges can help you set an initial budget:

- **Chain solder:** $30-$100+
- **Ring sizing:** $50-$175+
- **Prong repair:** $40-$125 per prong
- **Stone tightening:** $30-$100+
- **Clasp replacement:** $50-$250+
- **Rhodium plating:** $60-$150
- **Polishing:** $40-$150

The plus sign matters. It accounts for pieces that require unusual access, additional metal, multiple repair points, specialized finishing, or work around vulnerable stones. It also reflects the difference between repairing a simple everyday item and preserving a complex piece whose construction limits the usual methods.

Treat a range as a planning tool rather than a quote. Before approving work, ask what the estimate includes: labor, replacement metal or findings, cleaning, finishing, stone checks, and any applicable tax should all be clear. ## Why two similar repairs can cost differently

Jewelry is small, but the decisions involved are not. Price follows the material, structure, condition, and intended finish—not merely the name of the repair.

### Metal type and condition

Gold karat, silver, platinum, and mixed-metal pieces behave differently under heat and at the bench. The jeweler must choose compatible solder or filler, control heat, and preserve the surrounding surface. Platinum generally requires different handling from gold. White gold may need refinishing and rhodium plating after heat or abrasion changes its appearance.

Condition matters just as much as metal type. A chain can break because it caught on something, but it can also fail where repeated wear has thinned several links. Soldering the first opening without noting adjacent weakness may leave the piece vulnerable nearby. An inspection should distinguish an isolated break from broader wear.

### Stones and settings

Heat, vibration, cleaning solutions, and polishing compounds are not equally suitable for every gemstone. Some stones need to be removed, protected, or worked around. Pavé, channel-set stones, halos, and stones extending around a band can make access more demanding.

A loose stone may need only careful tightening. If the prongs are worn down, split, or missing, rebuilding or replacing them is a different job. The quoted unit price for prong work can also change when several prongs must be restored together or when the setting itself is distorted.

### Construction and access

A plain chain link is usually easier to reach than a break beside a gemstone station, hinge, or decorative element. Hollow jewelry requires particular restraint because its walls can be thin. Antique and handmade pieces may include old solder seams or components that react differently when heated.

Ring sizing illustrates the same point. A plain band with room to add or remove metal is not equivalent to an eternity band with stones around the full circumference. Engraving, milgrain, inlay, enamel, or a two-tone design can affect whether sizing is advisable and how the original appearance is restored.

### Finish and final appearance

Repair labor does not end when the structure holds. The repaired area may need filing, shaping, sanding, and polishing so it blends with the surrounding piece. Satin, brushed, hammered, oxidized, and high-polish finishes each call for a different final step.

Polishing itself is not automatically harmless. It removes a very small amount of surface metal, so repeated aggressive polishing is not appropriate for every older or finely detailed piece. A practical conversation should address whether you want a bright overall finish, a light cleanup, or minimal intervention.

### Parts and findings

Clasp replacement varies widely because “clasp” covers small spring rings, lobster clasps, box clasps, safety mechanisms, and precious-metal components in different weights. The part can represent a meaningful share of a $50-$250+ job. Matching the metal, dimensions, color, strength, and connection points may matter more than choosing the least expensive finding.

The same principle applies to chain sections, jump rings, earring posts, backs, and replacement stones. An estimate should identify whether a new part is included and, when relevant, whether it is being matched to the existing piece.

## What the common repairs involve

### Chain solder: $30-$100+

The jeweler aligns the broken area, makes a controlled joint, removes excess solder, and restores the finish. Additional breaks, very fine links, hollow construction, or a break close to a stone setting can raise the cost. If multiple links are thin, shortening the chain or replacing a section may be more sensible than repeatedly soldering isolated failures.

### Ring sizing: $50-$175+

Sizing may involve cutting the shank, adding or removing compatible metal, closing the seam, reshaping the ring, and refinishing it. The amount of size change, shank width, metal, stones, engraving, and plating all influence the plan. Some designs have a narrow sizing limit; others should not be sized conventionally at all.

### Prong repair and stone tightening

Prong repair typically runs $40-$125 per prong, while straightforward stone tightening may be $30-$100+. These are related but not interchangeable. Tightening adjusts sound metal to secure a stone. Repair adds or rebuilds metal where a prong no longer provides adequate coverage. Once one prong shows serious wear, the others deserve inspection too.

### Clasp replacement: $50-$250+

The total depends on the finding and the work needed to attach it. A larger solid-gold clasp generally costs more than a small base-metal component. Safety features, multiple strands, end caps, and worn chain ends can add work beyond swapping one part.

### Rhodium plating: $60-$150

Rhodium gives many white-gold pieces a bright white surface. The jewelry is normally cleaned and prepared before plating; scratches or previous repairs may require finishing first. Plating is a surface treatment and will wear over time, especially on rings that contact desks, handles, and other objects daily.

### Polishing: $40-$150

Cost depends on size, detail, existing scratches, finish, and whether stones or delicate areas limit machine access. Deep marks may not be appropriate to remove completely. Preserving contours, hallmarks, engraving, and crisp edges can be more important than chasing a uniformly new appearance.

## What happens during an inspection

A useful intake inspection should look beyond the stated complaint. Depending on the piece, the jeweler may examine:

- Metal stamps, prior seams, cracks, thinning, and distortion
- Prongs, channels, bezels, and signs of stone movement
- Chain links, jump rings, hinges, catches, and clasp function
- Stone characteristics that affect heat, cleaning, or handling
- Plating, engraving, texture, and other finish requirements
- The repair area’s access and the need for replacement parts

Bring the whole piece, including any loose stone or broken component, in a secure container. Explain when the problem appeared and whether another repair was performed nearby. If the item has important engraving or a finish you want preserved, point it out at intake.

## How to compare estimates responsibly

Price alone does not describe the scope. Ask each jeweler the same practical questions:

1. What condition caused the problem?
2. Is the proposed work a local repair or a larger rebuild?
3. Are parts, finishing, and stone checks included?
4. Could heat or polishing change nearby details?
5. Is there a reason not to proceed with the requested repair?
6. How should the piece be cared for afterward?

An honest answer may be that a repair is technically possible but not economical, or that a fragile area should be stabilized before cosmetic work. That is useful information, not an obstacle.
