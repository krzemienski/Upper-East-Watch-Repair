The useful answer to “How often should a watch be serviced?” is not a single number. Movement type matters, but so do age, daily use, storage, moisture exposure, and recent performance. A lightly worn quartz dress watch and a mechanical watch used every day have different needs. Combine a planning interval with regular observation rather than opening a healthy watch unnecessarily or waiting for a small problem to grow.

## Start With the Movement Type

The movement is the mechanism inside the case. Most watches are either quartz, powered by a battery or stored electrical charge, or mechanical, powered by a mainspring. Mechanical watches may be manually wound or automatic; both rely on many lubricated, interacting parts.

### Mechanical and Automatic Watches

A common planning window for a mechanical watch is roughly every five to eight years. That is a guideline, not a deadline. Some manufacturers advise a different interval for a particular caliber, so the watch’s own documentation should take priority when it is available.

Mechanical lubricants can migrate, thicken, dry, or become contaminated by wear particles. A watch may continue running while its amplitude declines or its rate becomes less stable. Inadequate lubrication can add wear to pivots and other components.

An older watch, an unknown service history, or a watch unused for years deserves assessment before heavy daily wear. Vintage pieces also require judgment about opening the case, sourcing parts, and preserving original components.

### Quartz Watches

Quartz watches generally do not need the same recurring overhaul schedule as mechanical watches. Their timekeeping is electronically regulated, but they are not maintenance-free.

Battery replacement is the routine need most owners encounter. Battery life varies with the movement and functions; alarms, chronographs, illumination, and other features can affect consumption. A stopped battery-powered watch should not be left indefinitely with an exhausted cell because leakage can damage the movement.

Beyond the battery, a quartz watch may need attention when it stops intermittently, loses time, advances in unusual jumps, or consumes batteries quickly. Dirt in the gear train, damaged contacts, or an obstruction may be involved. Repeatedly installing a battery without diagnosing rapid drain is not a durable solution. Solar and kinetic watches are also quartz-regulated, but they store energy in a rechargeable cell or capacitor. They should not automatically receive an ordinary disposable battery. If one no longer holds a charge after appropriate light exposure or motion, it needs model-appropriate diagnosis.

## Symptoms Matter More Than the Calendar

A service interval is a planning tool. The watch’s behavior is evidence. Arrange an inspection when you notice:

- A meaningful change in daily gain or loss
- Stopping despite adequate winding or normal wear
- A noticeably shorter power reserve
- Erratic seconds-hand movement or intermittent operation
- Grinding, scraping, rattling, or unusual resistance at the crown
- A crown that is difficult to wind, set, screw down, or pull out
- Condensation, fogging, or visible moisture beneath the crystal
- A loose rotor sensation in an automatic watch
- Chronograph hands that fail to start, stop, or reset correctly
- A battery that expires much sooner than expected

Moisture inside the case is urgent. Do not operate the crown or pushers, and do not wait for the fog to disappear on its own. Water can affect the dial, hands, movement, and internal metal surfaces even after visible condensation clears. Bring the watch to a qualified repairer promptly.

A single day of unusual timekeeping does not always mean the watch needs an overhaul. Magnetism, low power, an impact, or resting position can alter performance. Assessment separates a limited repair from a full service.

## Use, Environment, and Storage Change the Schedule

Two watches with the same movement can age differently. Consider how the watch actually lives.

### Daily Wear and Physical Exposure

A watch worn daily accumulates more winding cycles, rotor movement, temperature changes, and shocks than one reserved for weekends. Repeated impacts can be relevant even when the case looks clean.

Water exposure also matters. Gaskets age whether or not the movement is due for service. If a watch is regularly worn for swimming or around water, periodic water-resistance testing is more useful than assuming resistance lasts until the next overhaul. Water resistance is a condition at a point in time, not a permanent property.

### Storage Is Not the Same as Preservation

Leaving a mechanical watch in a drawer reduces running wear, but time still affects lubricants and seals. Store watches in a dry, temperate place away from strong magnetic fields and direct sunlight. Avoid damp bathrooms and wide temperature swings.

For a battery-powered watch, remove or replace an exhausted battery rather than storing the watch stopped for years. Solar watches generally benefit from appropriate periodic light exposure, following the maker’s instructions. A watch winder can keep an automatic watch running, but it is not required for most simple watches and does not replace maintenance; continuous operation also means continuous mechanical activity.

## Why One Schedule Cannot Fit Every Watch

Rigid advice overlooks important differences:

1. **Manufacturer guidance varies.** Caliber design, lubrication, sealing systems, and service recommendations are not identical.
2. **Complications add complexity.** Calendars, chronographs, alarms, and other functions may add parts and diagnostic considerations.
3. **Service history matters.** A documented recent overhaul is different from an inherited watch with no records.
4. **Condition matters.** Corrosion, impact damage, prior repairs, and parts availability affect the appropriate work.
5. **The owner’s use matters.** A daily-worn sports watch faces different conditions than an occasional dress watch.

The goal is not to maximize the number of services. It is to maintain reliable operation while avoiding preventable wear and unnecessary intervention. If the watch is performing normally and remains within the maker’s stated interval, monitoring may be enough. If symptoms appear, the calendar should not be used as a reason to postpone inspection.

## What a Proper Mechanical Service Usually Includes

“Service” can mean different things, so ask what is included before approving work. A complete mechanical movement service generally involves:

- Evaluating the watch’s condition and functions
- Removing the movement from the case
- Disassembling the movement
- Cleaning movement components
- Inspecting parts for wear, damage, or corrosion
- Replacing necessary worn or damaged parts, subject to availability
- Reassembling with suitable lubricants at specified points
- Regulating and testing the movement over time
- Cleaning the case and bracelet when appropriate and agreed
- Inspecting seals and testing water resistance when the construction permits
- Performing final operational and timekeeping checks

Polishing is separate and should be discussed, not assumed. Refinishing changes the metal surface and can soften edges or alter the appearance, particularly after repeated work. Many owners prefer cleaning without cosmetic refinishing.

Quartz service may be much narrower. A careful [battery replacement service](/services/battery-replacement) can include confirming the battery type, inspecting the compartment and contacts, checking movement function, examining the gasket, and discussing water-resistance testing. A quartz movement with a fault may require deeper diagnosis or replacement options.

## How to Prepare for an Assessment

Before visiting a watchmaker, note what the watch is doing and when it began. For a mechanical watch, record approximate daily gain or loss and whether the power reserve has changed. Bring previous service paperwork, replaced parts, warranty documents, and any model-specific instructions you have.

Ask practical questions:

- Is a complete service recommended, or is a limited repair sufficient?
- What testing supports that recommendation?
- Which parts may need replacement?
- Will cosmetic refinishing be performed only with approval?
- Is water-resistance testing appropriate for this case?
- How will additional work discovered after disassembly be authorized?

## A Practical Service Plan

Use the maker’s recommendation as the baseline, treat roughly five to eight years as a common planning range for many mechanical watches when no specific guidance is available, and let actual symptoms move the inspection earlier. For quartz watches, replace exhausted batteries promptly and investigate abnormal battery life or operation instead of relying on a universal overhaul cycle. In both cases, check water resistance according to use rather than waiting automatically for movement service.
